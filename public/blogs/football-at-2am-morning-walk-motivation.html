Football at 2 AM? No Problem. A Morning Walk at 6 AM? Maybe tomorrow.

By Gopi Krishna · September 2026

Living as a football fan in India often means adjusting our sleep schedules to accommodate matches that take place at unreasonable hours. A 2 AM kick-off can somehow feel entirely reasonable when it involves our favourite club. We stay awake, follow the line-up, anticipate the match, and sacrifice a few hours of sleep without thinking twice about it. Yet, when it comes to waking up at 6 AM for a morning walk, the same commitment can suddenly feel impossible.

The contrast is interesting. Why does sacrificing sleep for something we enjoy feel so easy, while finding the motivation to engage in a self-care routine can feel like such a struggle?

One way of understanding this difference is through motivation, effort, and reward.

When the reward feels worth the effort

Our willingness to put effort into an activity can be influenced by how rewarding we expect that activity to be. Research on sleep deprivation and effort allocation suggests that although tiredness can make effort feel more demanding, stronger motivation and incentives can increase our willingness to invest effort despite that tiredness.

Consider watching a football match at 2 AM. The moment we turn on the television, we are met with immediate excitement and anticipation. There is the possibility of seeing our favourite team win, watching a particular player perform and scoring a late winner and experiencing the unpredictability of the game, and perhaps sharing the experience with friends or other fans. The reward is immediate, emotionally engaging, and personally meaningful. The effort of staying awake may therefore feel like a reasonable price to pay.

A morning walk presents a very different reward structure. Getting out of bed at 6 AM involves effort: waking up, getting dressed, going outside, and walking for 30 or 40 minutes. The benefits of doing so—improved fitness, better physical health, and potentially greater wellbeing—are valuable, but they tend to accumulate gradually rather than appear immediately after one walk. Research has also found that sleepiness can influence motivation towards activities such as exercise. The effort is immediate. The reward is often delayed.

And that difference can influence how motivated we feel.

What we focus on can change how an activity feels

Another factor is the information we pay attention to when deciding whether to do something. Studies show that people may differ in whether they tend to seek information about the effort required or the reward available before making a decision. This preference can influence their willingness to engage in effortful activities. In particular, seeking information about effort has been associated with a reduced willingness to choose activities requiring greater effort (Müller et al., 2022).

Think about a 6 AM morning walk. Before even getting out of bed, we may start calculating the effort involved: getting dressed, stepping outside in the cold or heat, walking for 40 minutes, and then getting ready for the rest of the day. The activity can begin to feel exhausting before it has even started.

The thought process surrounding a 2 AM football match can look very different. Our attention may immediately go towards the reward: the excitement of the game, the possibility of a win, the anticipation of seeing our favourite players, and the conversations we will have about the match afterwards.

The effort of staying awake is still there. What changes is what captures our attention first. When the reward feels more salient than the effort, we may be more willing to tolerate the cost involved.

Not all motivation is the same

There is another important distinction to consider: the quality of our motivation.

Self-Determination Theory proposes that motivation can differ in quality. We may engage in an activity because we genuinely enjoy it or personally value it, or because we feel pressured to do it or believe that we simply should. This distinction is particularly relevant to behaviours such as exercise.

Consider someone who goes for a morning walk because they genuinely enjoy the quiet time, the feeling of being outdoors, or the sense of accomplishment afterwards. Compare this with someone who walks only because they feel guilty about not exercising. The behaviour may look identical from the outside, but the underlying motivation is very different.

Research examining motivation during physical endurance tasks found that people with greater autonomous motivation—motivation based on personal interest or value—were less tempted to reduce their effort and placed greater value on pursuing their goals. These processes were also associated with better performance. In simple terms, when we genuinely value or enjoy something, we may be more willing to tolerate the effort involved.

This may help explain why a football fan can stay awake until 2 AM despite knowing they will be tired the next day. The match carries enough personal value and enjoyment to make the sacrifice feel worthwhile. A morning walk may not yet hold the same immediate emotional significance.

What does this mean for self-care?

It is easy to interpret difficulty with exercise or other self-care routines as a lack of discipline. However, motivation is more complex than simply having or lacking willpower. We are constantly evaluating the effort required by an activity against what we expect to gain from it. When an activity is enjoyable, personally meaningful, socially rewarding, or immediately gratifying, we may be more willing to tolerate considerable effort.

This does not mean that we need to make every healthy behaviour exciting. Nor does it mean that long-term benefits are unimportant. Rather, it suggests that making an activity personally meaningful may make it easier to sustain. A morning walk could become more than simply “exercise.” It could be time to listen to a favourite playlist, catch up on a podcast, spend some time alone, explore a new route, or simply enjoy a quiet start to the day. The more personally meaningful the experience becomes, the less it may feel like something we are forcing ourselves to do.

Perhaps, then, the difference between staying awake until 2 AM for football and getting out of bed at 6 AM for a walk isn't simply about discipline. Sometimes, motivation depends on whether the reward feels worth the effort. And perhaps the question isn't just, “How can I make myself exercise?” It is: “How can I make this something worth showing up for?”
